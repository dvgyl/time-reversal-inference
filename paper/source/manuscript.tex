\begin{abstract}
Time asymmetry in a recording can arise from the physical source or from unequal detector responses. We determine when these explanations can be distinguished for stationary Gaussian sources observed through linear channels. If source frequencies and channel delays are unrestricted, unequal delays can make a reversible source reproduce the joint probabilities of any finite stationary Gaussian record. This holds when the recorded fluctuations have no exact linear constraint. Source inference therefore requires information beyond the record. We derive two finite-record tests that use such information. A calibrated two-channel test bounds the asymmetry allowed by relative detector phase. A three-channel spectral product cancels detector phase under a sampled diagonal response model. Both tests control false rejection using a bound on dependence across all channels and recorded times. The spectral test also needs a bound on covariances beyond the retained time separations. In a simulated Brownian gyrator at equilibrium, unequal sensors produce raw bootstrap detections in 397 of 400 records of length $N=524289$, while the calibrated source test rejects none. At the same length, the calibrated test detects all 200 records at a fourfold bath-temperature ratio but none at a twofold ratio. The tests thus separate detector-induced asymmetry from source evidence, with a substantial cost in recording length. A rotational example establishes the remaining physical limit: identical sampled laws can have unboundedly different continuous-time entropy-production rates.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
A particle at thermal equilibrium has no preferred direction of circulation. Yet two sensors that record its coordinates can produce a preferred time order if their responses differ. One sensor may respond immediately while the other retains a small contribution from an earlier position. The relation between the two recorded signals then depends on their time order, even though the particle remains at equilibrium (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensor-example}). More data make this recorded asymmetry easier to detect. They do not establish whether it belongs to the particle or to the measurement.

This distinction matters when fluctuations are used to identify physical activity. Detailed balance means that forward and reverse transitions balance in pairs. Violations have been detected in active biological systems~\cite{battle2016}. Trajectory asymmetry has also been used to estimate entropy production in actomyosin, a network of interacting actin and myosin proteins~\cite{seara2018}. Entropy production measures thermodynamic irreversibility. Current fluctuations and cycling frequencies provide related measures of nonequilibrium behavior~\cite{li2019,mura2018,gradziuk2019}. These methods connect statistical time order to physical driving under specified dynamical and observation assumptions~\cite{gonzalezarea2019,ohgaasymmetry2023,cerasolispectra2022,seara,gnesotto2018}. Unequal observation channels introduce another possible source of that order. Fluorescence-filter switching delays measurements at different wavelengths~\cite{stehbens2012}; photodiodes and electronics alter the temporal response of optical traps~\cite{bergsorensen2004}. Cross spectra express how two signals vary together at each frequency. Their phase describes relative timing, which these detector effects can change~\cite{tongzhang,bhandari2026}.

We ask when a finite recording rules out every reversible source allowed by the observation model. A reversible source has the same joint probabilities when time runs forward or backward. We study stationary Gaussian sources. Their statistical rules do not change with time, and their joint distributions are normal. Their means and covariances determine those distributions completely. This gives a precise setting in which to separate an ambiguity of the measurement from uncertainty caused by a finite sample. We allow stable linear responses that act separately on the channels, together with stationary Gaussian observation errors that are uncorrelated across channels and with the source.

The ambiguity is complete if source spectra and channel delays are unrestricted. We prove that any nonsingular finite stationary Gaussian law can arise from a reversible source observed through unequal delays. Thus no test with controlled false rejection over that class can distinguish an asymmetric alternative. The result does not depend on estimating the law poorly: it holds even when the finite-record probabilities are known exactly. Arbitrarily small suitable delays give the same obstruction when unrestricted continuous-time frequencies can alias under sampling.

Useful source inference must therefore restrict the possible measurement explanations. We develop two ways to do this. For two channels, calibration bounds the asymmetry that the relative response phase can create. With three channels, a product of pairwise cross spectra cancels detector phase under the sampled response model. Each operation constrains a quantity defined by the underlying probability law, called its population value. An estimate from one finite record can differ from that value. We then convert that constraint into a finite-record test by controlling dependent sampling errors. The required covariance bounds are part of the information supplied to the test, not consequences of phase calibration or cancellation.

The physical examples show both the value and the cost of these bounds. In the Brownian model, the calibrated test permits the measured equilibrium asymmetry and detects sufficiently strong driving. Its conservative covariance bound also prevents detection of a weaker driven source at every tested length. We examine this recording cost with known source spectra and with persistence estimated from a reference channel. Finally, we return to the distinction between a sampled trajectory and the underlying motion. A rotating Gaussian source can execute additional full turns between observations without changing its sampled law. Even successful detector correction cannot resolve the resulting ambiguity in entropy production.

\section{Discussion}
An asymmetric recording becomes evidence against a reversible source only after the allowed observation mechanisms have been constrained. The finite-law construction shows why this information is necessary. Calibration and spectral cycles then provide two distinct population constraints: one bounds the phase that the detectors can add, and the other cancels it. Neither constraint by itself supplies a finite-record decision. The complete covariance and, for the cycle, the omitted-lag bound determine whether the observed departure can exceed sampling uncertainty.

The Brownian example gives this distinction a physical interpretation. Its equilibrium records become easier to distinguish from a reversible \emph{recorded} process as length increases. The raw bootstrap detections are therefore expected under unequal sensors. The calibrated test asks a different question and retains the equilibrium explanation. Its failure to detect the twofold-temperature alternative at every tested length also matters. A valid source test can be too conservative to reveal actual driving. The recording-cost result establishes a dependence cost even with exact information, while the persistence examples show the further cost of learning a usable covariance envelope.

These results connect established operations to the same source question. Closure products remove local phase~\cite{thyagarajan2022}; Gaussian quadratic-form inequalities control dependent estimates~\cite{laurentmassart,hsukakadezhang,basumichailidis}; and autoregressive inversion gives confidence sets~\cite{dufour1990,dufourking1991,andrews1993}. Their combination must allow a nonzero detector contribution and cover the whole accepted source set. The two-sided construction improves several persistent conditions, but intersecting it with lag confidence changes detection in both directions because the error allocations change. A more informative confidence set does not automatically yield a more powerful final decision.

Comparisons must also preserve the object being tested. Bi and Calhoun's coherence witness concerns shared hidden input~\cite{bicalhoun2026}. Their multiplicative response model filters independent noise and shared input together, so response factors cancel under an independent-probe null. Our null permits correlated reversible sources. Raw lag and imaginary-coherency procedures concern recorded asymmetry instead. The near-boundary controls illustrate the consequence: raw procedures reject all $400$ records, while calibrated procedures reject none, even when detector-induced contrast reaches $99\%$ of its population tolerance (SM Sec.~S9.4). These outcomes answer different questions about the same records.

For experimental use, the first requirement is a justified observation model. The calibrated route needs a bound on relative response phase. The cycle route needs sampled diagonal factorization and errors uncorrelated between channels. Both need a full covariance bound from physical dynamics or a valid confidence procedure, and the cycle route also needs the covariance tail. These quantities determine a threshold that can be assessed before a nonzero statistic is given a source interpretation. The examples show why a record measured in many correlation times can still be too short when the available bounds are broad.

All guarantees here assume stationarity, joint Gaussian laws, stable linear responses, fixed channel identities and common reversal parity, and the stated covariance and error bounds. Source fitting further requires a noiseless scalar reference and shared autoregressive marginal persistence. Reference noise, non-Gaussian innovations, correlated detector errors, and underestimated bounds fall outside the corresponding guarantees. The sensitivity analyses cover only their tested conditions, and the physical examples are synthetic. Bounded recording corrections cover a valid rounding error; they do not make a finite random generator exactly Gaussian.

Finally, source reversibility at the sampling interval is distinct from continuous-time dissipation. Relative entropy between forward and reversed trajectories constrains dissipation under the required observation and dynamical assumptions~\cite{seifert2012,lucenteinference2022,ohgaasymmetry2023}. The aliased rotations show that identical sampled laws can nevertheless correspond to arbitrarily different entropy-production rates. Detector information resolves one source of ambiguity, while bandwidth or dynamical information is needed for the other. Within the stated model, the tests identify when detector effects and finite-record uncertainty no longer suffice to explain the observed time asymmetry.
