\section{How much data does inference require?}\label{sec:cost}
The Brownian and rotational examples required long records before their valid thresholds permitted detection. How much of this cost is unavoidable, and how much comes from conservative bounds? We first set aside calibration and source estimation. Even with exact responses and known marginal spectra, a directional signal can occupy a narrow frequency range that a short record resolves poorly.

Consider unit-variance Gaussian channels with identity responses and marginal spectral densities between $1/2$ and $\mathcal S/2$, where $\mathcal S\ge3$. We seek an absolute covariance contrast of at least $\gamma$, with $0<\gamma\le1/4$. The difficult alternative used below places its contrast in two narrow bands. The record covariance can be decomposed into modes, each a weighted combination of observed values. Their total overlap with the two bands is $N/(\mathcal S-1)$, which measures the effective number of modes that probe the directional signal. Narrow bands reduce this number, so many observations can still provide little evidence against a reversible source.

We quantify the distinction through the relative entropy between the two finite-record laws. If this quantity is too small, no test can attain the desired false-rejection and detection probabilities. This use of relative entropy concerns statistical distinguishability. The same measure applied to forward and reversed trajectories has a thermodynamic interpretation under the appropriate physical assumptions~\cite{roldan2010,seifert2012}.

\begin{theorem}[Recording cost]\label{thm:main-cost}
For each $(\mathcal S,\gamma)$ in this class, SM Sec.~S6 constructs a fixed reversible source and a fixed alternative with contrast $-\gamma$ and the same known marginal spectra. The necessary length inequality in SM Sec.~S6.3 applies to any test of this pair with false-rejection probability $\alpha$ and detection probability $1-\beta$, where $0<\alpha,\beta<1$ and $\alpha+\beta<1$. For the full class, the calibrated test with exact identity responses and $q=\mathcal S$ has false-rejection probability at most $0.05$ and detection probability at least $0.95$ whenever
\begin{equation}
 N\ge1024\mathcal S/\gamma^2.\label{eq:cost-uniform}
\end{equation}
At $\mathcal S=9$, $\gamma=0.1$, and $\alpha=\beta=0.05$, at least $5214$ samples are necessary for the fixed pair; direct evaluation of the sharper sufficient condition gives $129580$ samples for the full class.
\end{theorem}
Thus dependence can force thousands of samples even with exact responses. SM Sec.~S6, Theorems~S8--S9, gives the complete necessary inequality, its finite trace-defect bound, and the sufficient calculation. The information required by the two error rates is $(1-\beta)\log[(1-\beta)/\alpha]+\beta\log[\beta/(1-\alpha)]$. A record is necessarily too short if an upper bound on its available relative entropy is smaller. The sufficient-to-necessary length ratio is below $25$ in the worked case. It compares a class-wide sufficient bound with a fixed-pair lower bound, so it is not an optimal-length claim.
